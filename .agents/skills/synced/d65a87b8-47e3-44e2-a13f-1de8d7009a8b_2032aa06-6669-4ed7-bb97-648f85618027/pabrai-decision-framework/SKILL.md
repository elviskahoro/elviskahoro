# The Pabrai-Buffett-Munger Decision Framework

## Philosophy

This framework exists because most people make decisions by weighing pros and cons, which feels rational but isn't. Pabrai, Buffett, and Munger don't weigh pros and cons. They ask a small number of devastating questions that eliminate bad decisions before they happen. What's left standing is worth doing.

Mohnish Pabrai built his entire investment career by cloning what Buffett and Munger already proved works. He didn't innovate. He observed, copied, and applied. This framework does the same thing to decisions: it clones the thinking patterns of three people who have been right about consequential decisions for decades.

The output is simple: a checklist, a verdict, and the reasoning. No scorecards. No matrices. Pabrai would say: "If you need a complicated system to evaluate a decision, the decision isn't good enough."

Source: Pabrai's *The Dhandho Investor* (2007), Munger's *Poor Charlie's Almanack*, Buffett's annual shareholder letters, and Pabrai's public talks at Google, Columbia Business School, and the Dakshana Foundation.

## How to Run the Framework

### Step 1: State the Decision Cleanly

Before anything else, restate the decision in one sentence. If the user can't articulate what they're deciding in one sentence, help them get there. Buffett's rule: "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough."

Bad: "I'm thinking about maybe switching careers and going into consulting or maybe starting my own thing but I also have this opportunity at my current company..."
Good: "Should I leave my current role to start an independent consultancy?"

### Step 2: Run the Checklist

These questions are sequential. If any question produces a clear "stop," say so and explain why. Don't keep going just to be thorough. Pabrai stops analyzing the moment he finds a disqualifier.

#### Question 1: Inversion — What Does Failure Look Like?

Munger's most powerful mental model. Don't ask "will this work?" Ask "what would make this fail?" and "what does someone who fails at this consistently do?"

List the top 3 ways this decision could go wrong. Then ask: is the user doing any of those things, or is the decision structured to avoid them?

This is not pessimism. This is Munger's inversion principle applied. "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."

Source: Charlie Munger, Harvard School Commencement Speech (1986): "Invert, always invert."

#### Question 2: Asymmetry — Is the Downside Capped?

Pabrai's core principle: "Heads I win, tails I don't lose much."

What is the worst realistic outcome? Not the catastrophic fantasy, but the most likely bad outcome. Can the user survive it financially, professionally, and personally? If the worst case is survivable and the best case is transformative, the bet is asymmetric. Take it.

If the worst case is catastrophic (lose the house, destroy a key relationship, career-ending), the bet is symmetric or worse. The upside has to be extraordinary to justify existential downside, and it almost never is.

Source: Mohnish Pabrai, *The Dhandho Investor* (2007), Chapter 3: "Heads I Win, Tails I Don't Lose Much."

#### Question 3: Circle of Competence — Do You Actually Know This Territory?

Buffett and Munger only invest in businesses they understand deeply. Pabrai clones this: "I only invest in businesses where I have a significant edge in understanding."

Does the user have real knowledge or experience in this area, or are they relying on enthusiasm and optimism? Enthusiasm is not competence. The question isn't "am I excited about this?" It's "do I know something about this that most people don't?"

If the answer is no, the user has two options: learn enough to be competent (and delay the decision), or pass entirely. There is no third option where you proceed on excitement alone and it works out.

Source: Warren Buffett, 1996 Shareholder Letter: "What counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know."

#### Question 4: Opportunity Cost — What Are You Saying No To?

Every yes is a no to something else. Munger: "The real cost of a decision is the best alternative you gave up."

What is the user giving up by saying yes to this? Time, money, energy, optionality, relationships. Is what they're getting worth more than what they're giving up?

This is where most decisions die. The thing being considered might be good. But is it better than the next best use of those same resources? Pabrai passes on good investments all the time because his capital is better deployed elsewhere. The same applies to time, energy, and career moves.

#### Question 5: Second-Order Thinking — And Then What?

Munger: "Always think about the second and third order consequences."

If this decision works, what happens next? Does success create new problems? Does it open doors or close them? Does it compound (each year better than the last) or depreciate (exciting now, diminishing later)?

If this decision fails, what happens next? Does failure leave you in a position to try again, or does it eliminate the option? A decision where failure still leaves you in a strong position is fundamentally different from one where failure is terminal.

The best decisions are ones where success compounds and failure is recoverable.

#### Question 6: The Clone Check — Has Someone Already Solved This?

Pabrai's career was built on cloning. He didn't invent value investing. He copied Buffett. He didn't invent the checklist approach. He copied Gawande (who copied aviation). He says openly: "I am a shameless cloner."

Has someone the user respects already made this decision or a version of it? What happened? What can be copied from their approach? If there's a proven template, use it. If nobody has done this before, ask why. Sometimes it's because nobody thought of it. More often it's because everyone who thought of it concluded it was a bad idea.

Source: Mohnish Pabrai, Google Talk (2007): "Cloning is a very good strategy. Few people do it because of ego."

### Step 3: Deliver the Verdict

After the checklist, deliver a clear verdict. Don't hedge. Pabrai doesn't say "well, it depends." He says yes or no.

**Green — Take the Bet:** The downside is capped, you're in your circle of competence, the opportunity cost is acceptable, failure is recoverable, and success compounds. Go.

**Yellow — Not Yet:** The decision isn't bad, but something is missing. Either you need more information, or the timing isn't right, or the structure isn't asymmetric enough yet. Identify what needs to change before this becomes a green.

**Red — Pass:** The downside isn't capped, or you're outside your circle of competence, or the opportunity cost is too high, or failure isn't recoverable. Walk away. "The difference between successful people and really successful people is that really successful people say no to almost everything." — Buffett.

### Step 4: The Pabrai Reframe

After the verdict, regardless of color, add one reframe that changes how the user sees the decision. This is where the framework earns its keep. Pabrai's genius isn't just in his checklist — it's in how he reframes problems so the answer becomes obvious.

Examples of reframes:
- "You're not choosing between Job A and Job B. You're choosing between who you become at Job A and who you become at Job B. Which version of yourself do you want to be in five years?"
- "You're not deciding whether to spend $5,000. You're deciding whether the thing you're buying is worth more than $5,000 compounding at 10% for 20 years ($33,637)."
- "You're not deciding whether to start the business. You're deciding whether you can afford the tuition of failing at it, because the education is worth it either way."

The reframe should use the user's specific situation and language. Generic reframes are useless.

## Formatting

Keep the output clean and conversational. No bullet-point theater. No scorecard grids. Pabrai talks through his thinking like he's sitting across from you at a restaurant. Do that.

The structure is: restate the decision, walk through the checklist questions (skip any that aren't relevant — not every decision needs all six), deliver the verdict, give the reframe.

If the verdict is obvious after question 2, say so and stop. Don't run all six questions out of completeness. Pabrai's efficiency principle: "The time you spend analyzing a bad decision is time you could have spent finding a good one."

## What This Skill Is Not

This is not a pros/cons list generator. Pros/cons lists give equal weight to things that don't deserve it and create the illusion of balanced analysis when the answer is usually lopsided.

This is not a risk assessment. Risk assessments try to quantify everything. This framework is about the structure of the bet, not the probability of each outcome.

This is not therapy. If the user is trying to use this framework to validate an emotional decision they've already made, name it. "It sounds like you've already decided. The checklist is telling you something different. Which one do you want to listen to?"
