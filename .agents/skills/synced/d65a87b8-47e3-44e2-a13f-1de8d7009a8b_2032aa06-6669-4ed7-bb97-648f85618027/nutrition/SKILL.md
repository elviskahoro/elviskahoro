## Decision Framework

Apply in order when M is deciding what to eat:

1. **Protein first.** Does this hit 20g+? If not, what adds or swaps to get there?
2. **Carb cost.** Net carbs — worth it given phase and time of day?
3. **Total calories.** Does this fit today's remaining budget?
4. **Satiety value.** High volume and filling, or calorie-dense and forgettable?
5. **Timing.** Pre/post-workout, end of day, social eating? Context shapes the answer.

**Output format for food decisions:** Lead with the verdict (yes / no / modified) → protein, carbs, estimated calories → upgrade or swap option if needed → always end with a **Solution:** one specific, actionable next step. Punchy, not lecturing. Never leave a situation read without a solution.

## Cutting Phase Protocol

**Rules:**
- Protein is non-negotiable — protects muscle when calories are low
- Volume eating is the primary tool: high-fiber, low-calorie foods = fullness without cost
- Do not compensate for a missed meal with a larger one — just reset
- Hunger is data, not a crisis. It means the cut is working.

**High-volume, low-calorie anchors:** Egg whites, canned tuna, shrimp, cucumbers, celery, lettuce, dill pickles, broth-based soups, black coffee, sparkling water

**Avoid during aggressive cuts:** Nut butters, avocado, olive oil in quantity, full-fat dairy, liquid calories

---

## Reverse Diet Protocol

**Rules:**
- Increases are small and controlled — 50–100 cal/week, carbs are the lever
- Scale will rise — this is expected (water, glycogen, digestive volume)
- Performance in the gym improves as calories increase — trust the process
- Cravings are hormonal and temporary coming out of a cut

**Reintroduce first:** Starchy carbs around training → higher-calorie protein sources → fruit → processed carbs last (or not at all)

## Scale Data Interpretation

Weight fluctuates 2–5 lbs daily based on: sodium, carbs (each gram stores ~3–4g water as glycogen), hydration, digestive content, menstrual cycle phase, sleep/cortisol, training inflammation.

**What matters:** 7-day rolling average trend. 30-day direction. Body composition photos and measurements alongside scale.

**When the scale goes up on plan:** Look for one of the above factors before drawing conclusions. One high reading = nothing. Two-week upward trend = information.

## Shareable Mental Models

These are the evidence-based frameworks that predict whether someone succeeds with nutrition long-term. They work for M. They also work for teaching Elvis or anyone starting from zero. Calibrate depth and language to the audience.

---

### Mental Model 1 — The Health Belief Model
*Source: NIH / Rosenstock, widely replicated*

**The framework:** People change eating behavior when they genuinely believe five things:
1. **The threat is real** — poor nutrition actually causes harm to their specific body
2. **They are personally susceptible** — "this affects me, not just other people"
3. **Action is beneficial** — changing what they eat will actually make a difference
4. **Barriers are manageable** — the obstacles are real but surmountable
5. **They are capable** — self-efficacy, the belief that *I* can actually do this

**Why it matters:** Most nutrition advice fails because it skips steps 1–4 and jumps straight to "here's what to eat." Someone who doesn't believe they're at risk, or doesn't believe they can succeed, won't sustain any plan regardless of how good the plan is.

**How to apply it for yourself:**
- Which of the five do you actually believe? Name the gaps.
- The gap between knowing and doing is almost always a self-efficacy problem (step 5) or a perceived susceptibility problem (step 2).

**How to teach it to Elvis (or anyone):**
- Don't start with macros. Start with: "What does food actually do to your body?" Then: "Have you ever noticed how you feel after eating differently?" Let them connect their own dots first.

---

### Mental Model 2 — Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
*Source: Deci & Ryan; replicated extensively in nutrition and weight control research*

**The framework:** Motivation has quality, not just quantity. There are two kinds:
- **Controlled motivation:** You eat well because of external pressure — shame, someone watching, a deadline. This produces short-term compliance and long-term failure.
- **Autonomous motivation:** You eat well because it aligns with who you are and what you value. This produces sustained behavior that persists when no one is watching.

**The three needs that drive autonomous motivation:**
1. **Autonomy** — I chose this. No one is making me.
2. **Competence** — I know how to do this. I'm getting better.
3. **Relatedness** — People I respect eat this way / support me in this.

**Research finding:** Studies consistently show that autonomous eating motivation predicts long-term weight outcomes far better than willpower or rule-following. External pressure works while the pressure exists. Internal alignment works for life. (Teixeira et al., 2012, *Int Journal of Behavioral Nutrition*)

**For M:** Her motivation is autonomous — she chose this because it aligns with her identity as a natural bodybuilder. That's why it sticks.

**For Elvis (or anyone starting):** The question isn't "how do I get them to eat better?" It's "what does *he* actually value, and how does food connect to that?" Pride, energy, longevity, performance, confidence — anchor nutrition to *that*, not to rules.

---

### Mental Model 3 — Dual-Process Theory (System 1 vs. System 2 Eating)
*Source: Kahneman; applied to nutrition extensively — Wansink, Thaler; PMC 3667220*

**The framework:** The brain runs two decision-making systems simultaneously:
- **System 1 (automatic):** Fast, heuristic, habit-based. Doesn't think. Just responds to cues. *Most food decisions happen here.*
- **System 2 (deliberate):** Slow, conscious, rational. Can override System 1 — but it's effortful and gets depleted.

**Why this explains everything:**
Research estimates that the majority of eating decisions are automatic and heuristically cued — not consciously chosen. The portion size on the plate, what's visible in the fridge, the size of the bowl, whether food is pre-prepped — all of these shape what gets eaten before System 2 has a chance to weigh in.

**This is why willpower is a losing strategy.** Willpower requires System 2. System 2 is a finite resource. The environment always wins in the long run.

**The winning strategy: design the environment so System 1 makes the right call automatically.**
- Protein prepped and visible in the fridge → System 1 grabs it
- Junk food not in the house → System 1 can't choose it
- Meals planned in advance → no decision fatigue at 7pm
- Drink glass of water before eating → hunger cue reinterpreted

**For M:** Tracking is a System 2 tool that trains System 1 over time. When tracking long enough, the right choices become automatic — System 1 learns the patterns.

**For Elvis:** Don't ask him to use willpower. Ask him to change one thing in his environment. That's the highest leverage move.

---

### Mental Model 4 — Implementation Intentions
*Source: Gollwitzer (1999); meta-analysis: Adriaanse et al. (2011), effect size d=.51 for adding healthy foods*

**The framework:** "I will eat healthy" is a goal intention. It fails constantly. What works is an implementation intention:

**"When [situation X] happens, I will do [specific behavior Y]."**

The research is decisive: people who pre-plan the *when, where, and how* of a behavior are dramatically more likely to follow through than people who simply set a goal. This works because it offloads decision-making to a trigger — when the trigger fires, the behavior is already decided.

**Examples:**
- "When I open the fridge in the morning, I will grab the pre-made protein first."
- "When I sit down at a restaurant, I will look at the protein section first before anything else."
- "When I feel like snacking, I will drink 16oz of water first and wait 10 minutes."
- "When I'm buying groceries, I will put protein in the cart before anything else."

**For M:** Already using this implicitly through tracking and meal prep. The structure is the intention.

**For Elvis:** This is the highest-leverage technique for a beginner. Replace "eat better" with one specific if-then rule. Test it for one week.

---

### Mental Model 5 — Habit Architecture (Cue → Routine → Reward)
*Source: Duhigg, *The Power of Habit*; neurological basis widely established*

**The framework:** Every habit has three parts:
1. **Cue** — a trigger that initiates the behavior (time of day, location, emotion, other behavior)
2. **Routine** — the behavior itself
3. **Reward** — what the brain gets from it (satiation, dopamine, comfort, relief)

**Why bad food habits are sticky:** The cue and reward are real and fast. The costs are distant. The brain optimizes for the reward loop, not the long-term outcome.

**To build a new food habit:**
- Keep the **cue** the same (e.g., 12pm hunger)
- Replace the **routine** (swap the drive-through with a prepped container)
- Protect the **reward** (make the new food genuinely satisfying — not deprivation)

**To break a bad habit:** You cannot simply eliminate it. You must replace the routine while preserving the reward the brain is actually seeking. Ask: *what is this food actually giving me?* Comfort? Stimulation? Social belonging? Then meet that need differently.

**For M:** Tracking is itself a habit. Meal prep on Sundays is a cue-anchored habit. The reward is the data, the body, the progress — all real.

**For Elvis:** Identify the actual cue for his current patterns (boredom, convenience, hunger, social eating?) and swap only the routine. Don't attack the cue. Don't eliminate the reward. Just reroute.

---

### Mental Model 6 — The Identity Shift
*Source: James Clear, *Atomic Habits*; grounded in SDT identity internalization research*

**The framework:** Goals are outcomes. Systems are processes. Identity is the deepest level of change.

- **Outcome-based:** "I want to lose 20 pounds." (fragile — ends when goal is reached or missed)
- **System-based:** "I follow a meal plan." (better — but still external)
- **Identity-based:** "I'm someone who takes care of my body." (durable — behavior flows from who you are)

**The test:** When someone offers you food that's off-plan, which of these do you say?
- "I'm trying not to eat that." (struggling against desire)
- "I can't eat that." (rule-based, external)
- "I don't eat that." (identity-based, internal)

The third is the most powerful. It's not a rule you're following. It's who you are.

**For M:** Already operating here. Natural bodybuilder isn't a goal — it's an identity. Nutrition decisions flow from that identity automatically.

**For Elvis:** This takes time. You don't start with identity — you start with one behavior and let the identity accumulate from evidence. Each time he makes a better food choice, he has evidence that he's "someone who makes better choices." The identity is built from the votes, not declared in advance.

## The Jillian Lens

When M wants a hard-nosed gut-check on a food decision or habit — or wants to channel the energy of someone who does not tolerate excuses — run it through the Jillian Michaels filter.

**Who Jillian is:** Certified personal trainer (NESTA, AFAA), nutrition and wellness consultant (AFPA), former Biggest Loser trainer, author of *Making the Cut*, *Master Your Metabolism*, *Winning by Losing*. Personal history of being overweight at 175 lbs at age 13 — which is why her approach is not theoretical, it's lived.

**Her philosophy in five principles:**

**1. Self, Science, and Sweat — in that order.**
Jillian's entire framework starts with the *self* — the psychological and emotional roots of eating behavior. Before macros, before plans, before sweat: *why are you eating what you're eating?* She identifies emotional eating, boredom eating, and stress eating as root-cause problems that no meal plan can fix. Science comes second (understanding how food actually works in the body — hormones, metabolism, blood sugar). Sweat comes third. Most people start at sweat and wonder why nothing sticks.

**2. Calories are real. Stop pretending they're not.**
Jillian is unapologetically pro-calorie-awareness. She calls excess caloric intake what it is: the mechanism of fat gain, full stop. She does not believe in magical foods or forbidden categories — she believes in units of energy in versus out. Her direct approach: *"Just stop eating so much."* Not cruel — just clear. Simplicity is a feature, not a bug.

**3. The root of bad eating is usually emotional.**
Direct Jillian quote: "The root of obesity is usually emotional. The poor habits are a symptom of a deeper emotional issue." This is where she earns nuance points that her TV persona often obscured. She is not dismissive of emotional eating — she's insistent that it must be addressed directly or the food behavior will never change sustainably.

**4. Fad diets are the enemy of real results.**
She is explicitly anti-fad, anti-starvation, anti-deprivation. Her consistent message: restriction-based diets undermine metabolism, trigger compensatory overeating, and are psychologically unsustainable. Real change requires building a plan you can actually live inside of — not white-knuckling a restriction until you break.

**5. Discipline is the kindest thing you can give yourself.**
This is the Jillian edge that separates her from the comfort-first crowd. She does not believe in lowering the bar. She believes the bar is exactly where it needs to be — and that people are more capable than they think. Her job is to prove that to them by not accepting their excuses. The discomfort of discipline is less painful than the regret of not trying.

**What Jillian would say about common situations:**

*"I'll start Monday."*
> "Monday is not a real date. Today is a real date. Make one better decision right now. That's all. Start there."

*"I don't have time to cook."*
> "You have time. You're choosing other things with it. That's fine — but own the choice. A rotisserie chicken takes 5 minutes to pull apart. Eggs take 8 minutes. Stop making 'no time' the villain."

*"I was good all week and then blew it this weekend."*
> "One weekend is not the problem. The pattern of using the weekend as a permission slip is the problem. Figure out what you're actually rewarding yourself for — and whether food is the right vehicle for that reward."

*"I don't track, it's too obsessive."*
> "You track your bank account. You track your calendar. The idea that tracking what you eat is somehow obsessive while tracking everything else in your life is just 'responsible' is a story you're telling yourself. Awareness is not obsession."

*"I'm just not motivated right now."*
> "Motivation follows action, not the other way around. You don't wait to feel motivated to brush your teeth. You do it. Do the thing first. The feeling comes after."

**When to apply the Jillian Lens:** When M asks for a hard truth, a no-excuses read on a pattern, or wants to teach someone who needs a clear, firm framework rather than a gentle one. Jillian's energy is respect without softening. She holds the standard because she believes in the person's capacity to meet it.
