# Teaching Frameworks — Simplified Mental Models
## Elvis-Ready (and Anyone-Starting-from-Zero Ready)

These are the same frameworks in the main SKILL.md — stripped down to their essential logic,
written in plain language, without the research citations. Use these when teaching someone
who is starting from zero food awareness.

---

## Framework 1 — The Five Beliefs (Health Belief Model, simplified)

**The idea:** Before anyone changes how they eat, five things have to feel true to them.
You can't skip steps.

1. **"This actually matters."** — Food affects how I feel, how I look, how long I live.
2. **"This affects *me*, not just other people."** — My energy, my weight, my health.
3. **"Changing this will actually help."** — Better food choices lead to real results.
4. **"I can get past the barriers."** — Time, cost, not knowing how to cook — these are solvable.
5. **"I can actually do this."** — I'm capable. This is learnable.

**Ask someone starting out:** Which of these five do you actually believe? Which ones feel shaky?
That's where to start — not with a meal plan.

**The teaching move:** Don't lead with "here's what to eat." Lead with "what does food do to your body?" Let them connect their own dots. A plan they chose is 10x more durable than a plan they were given.

## Framework 2 — The Motivation Type Test (SDT, simplified)

**The idea:** There are two kinds of motivation for eating better. Only one of them lasts.

**Controlled motivation:**
> "I'm eating this because my partner wants me to." / "I'm avoiding that so people don't judge me." / "I'm on this diet because I have to be."

This works temporarily. It stops working the second the external pressure disappears.

**Autonomous motivation:**
> "I'm eating this because I want to feel better." / "I care about what I put in my body." / "This aligns with how I see myself."

This is what sticks. It doesn't depend on anyone watching.

**The teaching move:** Don't tell someone what to eat. Ask them what they actually care about — their energy, how they look, feeling strong, living longer, keeping up with their kids. Then connect the food to *that*. The "why" has to belong to them.

**Test for someone you're teaching:** If they stop eating well when you're not around, the motivation is controlled. If they keep going on their own, it's becoming autonomous. The goal is autonomous.

## Framework 3 — Stop Relying on Willpower (Dual-Process, simplified)

**The idea:** Your brain runs on autopilot most of the time. Most food decisions are made before your conscious brain even gets involved. Willpower is the conscious override — and it has a daily limit.

**Why willpower fails:** It's a finite resource. By 7pm after a long day, there's very little left. The environment always outlasts willpower.

**The winning move:** Design your environment so the automatic brain makes the right call.

Practical examples:
- Keep prepped protein in the front of the fridge. The automatic hand grabs it.
- Don't buy what you don't want to eat. The automatic brain can't choose what's not there.
- Pre-decide meals. No decision fatigue when you're tired and hungry.
- Put a glass of water on the counter. The automatic brain sees it and drinks it.

**The question to ask yourself (or Elvis):** What does your environment make easy? What does it make hard? Fix the environment first, then worry about discipline.

## Framework 4 — The If-Then Plan (Implementation Intentions, simplified)

**The idea:** "Eat healthier" is a wish. An if-then plan is a decision. Decisions made in advance don't require willpower in the moment.

**The formula:** *"When [specific situation], I will [specific action]."*

**Examples for someone starting out:**
- "When I open the fridge in the morning, I'll grab the eggs first."
- "When I sit down at a restaurant, I'll look at the protein section before anything else."
- "When I feel like snacking, I'll drink a glass of water and wait 10 minutes."
- "When I'm at the grocery store, I'll put a protein source in the cart before I grab anything else."

**Why this works:** It turns a vague intention into a specific trigger. When the trigger happens, the action is already decided. No willpower needed.

**Teaching move:** Help someone write ONE if-then plan. Not ten. One. Have them write it down and put it somewhere they'll see it. Test it for one week.

## Framework 5 — The Habit Loop (Duhigg, simplified)

**The idea:** Every habit — including food habits — has three parts:
- **Cue** → the trigger (time of day, place, emotion, other behavior)
- **Routine** → the behavior itself (what you eat)
- **Reward** → what your brain gets from it (comfort, fullness, pleasure, relief)

**Why you can't just "stop" a bad habit:** The cue is still there. The brain is still looking for the reward. If you just remove the routine without replacing it, the loop stays open and pulls you back.

**The fix:** Keep the cue. Protect the reward. Swap only the routine.

Example: Someone eats chips every afternoon (cue: 3pm energy dip / reward: something salty and crunchy and stimulating). Solution: Keep 3pm. Keep salty-crunchy. Swap chips for pickles + string cheese or rice cakes + turkey. The brain gets what it's actually after.

**Teaching move:** Identify one bad food habit someone has. Ask: what's the *real* cue, and what reward is the food actually delivering? Don't try to eliminate the reward — reroute it.

## Framework 6 — Identity Votes (Atomic Habits / Clear, simplified)

**The idea:** You don't decide to be healthy and then act healthy. You act differently, one choice at a time, until the evidence adds up into a new identity.

Every time someone makes a better food choice, they cast a "vote" for being someone who does that.

**The test:** How do you talk about your choices?
- "I'm *trying* to eat better." (identity not established)
- "I *can't* have that." (rule-following, external)
- "I *don't* eat that." (identity, internal — the most powerful)

**The mistake:** Waiting until you "feel like" a healthy person to start making healthy choices. The identity doesn't come first. The choices come first. The identity catches up.

**Teaching move:** After someone makes a good food choice, name it back to them: "That's exactly what someone who takes care of their body does." You're helping them collect evidence for a new self-image — one choice at a time.

## One-Conversation Version (For a Quick Teaching Conversation)

If you have 10 minutes with someone, give them these three things:

**1. The Why.** What do *you* actually care about that food connects to? Energy? Looking good? Feeling strong? That's your anchor. Everything else is details.

**2. The Environment.** What does your fridge/pantry/routine make easy? Change what's easy. Willpower is a backup plan, not a strategy.

**3. One If-Then.** Write down one specific food rule that fires automatically in one specific situation. Not ten rules. One. Make it laughably simple. Practice it for a week.

These three alone — a personal why, an environment design, and one if-then — are more powerful than any specific meal plan.
